Research

What I work on

AMSE Lab
Dec 2025 – present

Porous PVA brushes for post-CMP cleaning

A cleaning brush works only if its pores are the right size, connected, and held by walls that survive drying. I studied how our lab's starch-based synthesis forms pores and tried sugar and mannitol as alternatives. Cross-sectional SEM showed that particle-templated brushes formed weak walls that shrank during drying, which pointed to the templating method itself. I am now exploring spinodal decomposition, where pores form from within a uniform solution.

AMSE Lab

Polyimide CMP for hybrid bonding

Polyimide's chemical stability is what makes it useful for hybrid bonding, and also what makes it hard to polish. I work on tribochemical polishing with an iron nitrate-based slurry and on Cu/polymer planarization for advanced packaging. Early on, I saw a pad softer than the wafer scratch it with only deionized water, a result I still want to explain.

AMSE Lab

Conformal 3D printing of electroadhesive grippers

On a Jubilee tool-changing printer, the nozzle height shifted after every tool change. I built a system that sets each tool's z-offset automatically with a contact probe and a microscope, and it now runs as part of the printer. I also measured extrusion pressure across rates to tame the nonlinear flow of silver ink for direct ink writing.

How I do research

  1. ObserveLook closely at the structure that came out.
  2. TraceFollow it back to its processing history.
  3. ChangeAdjust the process, not just the material.
  4. Observe againTreat surprises as reasons to rethink.

I learn best by making things myself. I understand a process when I have run it, examined what it produced, and traced what went wrong back to how it was made.

Outside the lab

The science in ordinary things

I enjoy finding the science inside things most people use without a second thought. A macaron, a kitchen knife, and a sponge each hold decisions about structure that someone made, or that the process made for them. Asking why an everyday object works, and why it sometimes fails, is how most of my questions begin.

Baking

Twenty failed batches

I once wanted to be a pâtissier. It took me twenty failed batches to bake a macaron I was proud of. Some collapsed, some cracked, some ran across the tray. A macaron is a foam dried and heat-set into a porous structure, which turned out to be close to what I now do in the lab.

Metals

Why some knives stay sharp

Reading about why some knife steels hold an edge longer than others, together with a course on deformation and fracture, is how I became curious about metals and the microstructures that decide how they fail.

Edge micrograph of an OLFA blade from Science of Sharp.
